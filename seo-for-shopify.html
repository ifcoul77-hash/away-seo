In short: Shopify handles the technical basics of SEO properly. The risks sit elsewhere: collection filters, especially when an app generates them, theme templates, the weight of installed apps, and Product markup that drifts out of sync. This guide covers five checks, with what the documentation establishes and what you still need to verify on your own store.

Shopify is not a separate kind of SEO, but it has sensitive spots

A Shopify store follows the same ranking rules as a WordPress site or a custom build. What changes is where mistakes tend to hide: the collection and filter system, the theme templates, and the app ecosystem. A generic SEO checklist often misses these three areas because it does not know where to look inside Shopify.

Five checks to run on your store

1. Filter URLs on collection pages

When a visitor filters a collection by color, size or price, the page address changes. A collection with a handful of filters can quietly produce a large number of URL variants for almost identical content.

What Shopify already does. According to several practitioner analyses (we did not find an official Shopify page that spells it out), the default robots.txt already blocks sort parameters and tag combinations, and pages filtered with the native filtering point their canonical tag to the clean collection URL. Treat this as something to confirm on your store, not to assume.

Where it gets complicated. A third-party filter app, a modified theme or internal links pointing to filtered URLs can change that behavior.

Documented by Google. If you do not need faceted navigation URLs to be indexed, Google recommends preventing their crawling with robots.txt. The canonical link element and the nofollow attribute are described as other ways to signal a preference (Google Search Central, faceted navigation).

Our recommendation. List the URL parameters your filters really generate, apps included. Open the source of a filtered page and read the canonical it outputs. Then decide filter by filter: blocked, canonicalized, or turned into a real collection page when real search demand justifies it. Shopify lets you customize robots.txt through a dedicated robots.txt.liquid template.

2. Identical collection and product descriptions

It is tempting to leave every collection with the same one line introduction, and to reuse the manufacturer's text across every variant of a product. The risk is twofold: pages that look too alike to justify one ranking each, and descriptions that ten other stores publish word for word.

There is no numeric rule here, so we present this as a recommendation, not as a documented requirement. A text that only you can write (real use, a comparison, sizing or care constraints) gives a page a reason to exist that the manufacturer's copy does not.

Our recommendation. Give each collection its own introduction. Give each product at least one paragraph the manufacturer did not write. Start with the pages that bring in the most revenue.

3. Title tags and meta descriptions

By default, Shopify builds a title from the product or collection name followed by your store name. That works until dozens of pages end up with near identical titles, or titles too long to display in full. The fields to customize exist in the admin, on every product, collection and page.

This is a small job with a visible payoff, so start with your ten most important pages. Aim for roughly 60 characters: display depends on pixel width, and search engines may rewrite a title anyway. Bing's webmaster tools flagged overly long titles on an earlier version of this very guide, which is why we insist on it.

4. App weight and Core Web Vitals

Every installed app can load its own JavaScript: review widget, upsell popup, live chat, tracking tool. Depending on how it is integrated, that code may load on every page, including pages that do not need it. The number of apps matters less than what each one loads, and when.

Our recommendation. Measure Core Web Vitals on real pages (home, collection, product), favoring field data when it exists. Then test the effect of removing one app at a time, on a copy of the theme. Remember leftovers too: an uninstalled app can leave code behind in the theme.

5. Product structured data

Most Shopify themes include basic Product markup. It can still break or contradict itself when an app changes the price, the variants or the review display, or when the theme and an app each publish their own markup.

Documented by Google. Product markup makes a product page eligible for rich results such as price, availability and reviews (Google Search Central, Product structured data).

Our recommendation. Test your product pages with Google's Rich Results Test, compare the marked up price and availability with what the page displays, and look for duplicate Product blocks in the source code.

A concrete checklist

  • Read the canonical in the source of a filtered collection page,
  • Check the robots.txt your store actually serves,
  • Rewrite the titles and meta descriptions of your ten most important pages,
  • Give your top collections and products text that is really yours,
  • Measure Core Web Vitals on real pages, then test removing one app at a time on a copy of the theme,
  • Run your product pages through Google's Rich Results Test and compare the marked up price with the displayed one.

Frequently asked questions

Does Shopify have built-in SEO problems compared with WordPress?

Not inherently. Shopify handles the technical basics (sitemaps, HTTPS, mobile rendering) by default. The recurring problems come from how collections, filters and themes are set up, not from the platform itself.

Can you fix Shopify SEO without a developer?

Some fixes (titles, meta descriptions, alt text, collection descriptions) are done directly in the Shopify admin. Others (canonical rules on filtered URLs, structured data, theme performance) usually require changes to the theme code.

Which Shopify apps most often slow a store down?

Any app that injects its own JavaScript on every page, such as review widgets, upsell popups or trackers, adds render-blocking weight. What each app loads on pages that do not need it matters more than the number of apps.
